*Agentic AI systems currently acquire their tools by injection: every connected capability is serialized into the model's context, where it competes with the task itself for attention. This paper argues that tool knowledge belongs in a governable structure outside the model (a knowledge graph over the MCP registry, queried through a deliberately simple interface), and that the intelligence of an agentic system lives in the traversal policy over that structure rather than in the structure or the model alone. The argument runs in three layers: a philosophical framing in which problem-solving skill is a traversal policy over shared cognitive mechanisms; an architecture (the trialogue) that distributes that policy across a human authority, an orchestrating agent, and an independent adversary; and a class of systems here called neural-structural, in which a discrete graph and a continuous representation co-evolve under a shared learning signal. The paper closes with a preregistered experiment: if the central claim is right, graph-mediated tool provisioning should flatten the performance gap between frontier-scale and sub-billion-parameter models. The prediction is falsifiable, and what failure would look like is stated in advance.*

> **Status.** Working draft. The experiment in Section 6 is preregistered here; execution, results, and the accompanying repository will follow in a companion paper.

## 1. Framing: change, policy, and the meta-problem

Why is anyone good at solving problems? For me the honest answer has never been domain knowledge, and it has never been any particular method. Every architecture I have designed and every implementation I have shipped reduces, on inspection, to the same three ingredients: meta-problem solving, intent, and effort. The architectures are manifestations; the meta-problem is the constant. This paper takes that reduction seriously and asks what it would mean to build systems around it.

The claim, stated carefully: I have the same functional cognitive mechanisms as anyone else. What differs is the path taken through them. Treat cognition, loosely, as a pseudo-fuzzy state machine. At each node a decision is made, consciously or otherwise, about what to do next: retrieve or reason, reframe or persist, delegate or dig deeper, stop or continue. Skill is the tuning of the decision logic at those nodes. Tuned one way, the system is efficient at abstract problem solving in general; tuned another way, at diagnosis, or negotiation, or wayfinding. No tuning dominates all problems. Emerson had the essential observation: every person is my superior in some respect, and in that I learn from them. Effectiveness is always relative to a subset of problems, which means that a population's diversity of policies is a feature to be preserved, not noise to be averaged away. This principle recurs throughout the paper, at the scale of agents and again at the scale of tools.

Two consequences follow. First, if the difference between problem solvers is policy rather than hardware, then policy can be externalized. A harness can encode a traversal policy, instrument it, compare it against alternatives, and improve it. That is the license for everything below. Second, the claim sets its own evidence bar. A traversal policy that cannot be observed is an introspective story, and introspective stories are cheap. The architecture in this paper therefore treats the logging of decision nodes (query, ruminate, reframe, delegate, stop) as a first-class requirement, and the paper ends with a prediction that is permitted to fail in public.

There is an older idea underneath this framing: metanoia, the Greek term for a genuine change of mind, taken up across philosophy and psychology to distinguish transformation from its imitation. Change that is earned through confrontation becomes durable structure; change that is performed (a new shape adopted without a shift in underlying disposition) decays [1, 2]. Section 4 returns to it as an engineering requirement rather than a metaphor: structural mutation without a coupled change in the system's continuous representation is performed change, and it fails in the same way.

## 2. The trialogue

The harness distributes the traversal policy across three roles. Three roles, not three agents: each AI role may spawn arbitrarily many subagents, and the role structure is what remains fixed as the population scales.

The **human** is the authority and manager. The human sets overall direction, approves decisions above a value-and-impact threshold, and fields the questions that the system judges critical. The human's comparative advantage is not speed or recall; it is the ownership of intent, which under this framing is one of the three irreducible ingredients.

The **orchestrator** (the first AI) determines how to realize the human's goals. It decomposes work and tasks subagents with individual, bounded assignments. Critically, the orchestrator is the agent connected to the tool graph described in Section 3, and it provisions tools to subagents not by injecting full inventories into their contexts but by modulating access: adjusting edge weights over the graph and passing each subagent a projection containing only what its task requires. Whether provisioning authority should live entirely in the orchestrator is an open architectural question, flagged honestly as such.

The **adversary** (the second AI) exists to prevent the orchestrator from becoming an unexamined monoculture. It operates in three modes: malicious, in the red-team sense, probing for exploitable failure; contrarian, forcing the orchestrator to defend or adapt its positions; and perspectival, injecting a genuinely different way of seeing the problem to raise the diversity of the system's thinking. The adversary may spawn its own subagents.

Two independence requirements make the adversary real rather than decorative. First, the adversary maintains its own traversal weights over the shared tool graph. If its tool access flowed through the orchestrator, the orchestrator could starve its own critic; a red team provisioned by its target is a compliance exercise. Second, the adversary holds a direct escalation channel to the human. This addresses a principal-agent problem inherent in the orchestrator's design: someone must estimate the value and impact of decisions to determine what reaches the human for approval, and an orchestrator that self-estimates controls its own oversight. The adversary therefore doubles as an escalation auditor, empowered to tattle.

[[FIGURE 1: The trialogue. Three fixed roles over an arbitrary agent population. The orchestrator and adversary hold independent traversal weights over the shared tool graph, and the adversary's escalation channel to the human bypasses the orchestrator entirely.]]

The subagent pattern itself is a response to three observed constraints: single agents degrade when asked to address many tasks at once, past a threshold that appears to be model-bound [CITE: multi-task degradation literature]; context windows and compute are finite; and context pollution, the accumulation of task-irrelevant material in a model's working context, measurably erodes performance. Subagents are one mechanism by which the trialogue keeps each context small, scoped, and clean.

*A note on scope: nothing above over-specifies the internal architecture of any agent. An orchestrator, adversary, or subagent may internally use retrieval augmentation, knowledge-graph memories of its own, or private MCP processes unknown to the larger system. The trialogue constrains roles, provisioning, and escalation; it deliberately does not constrain implementations.*

## 3. Neural-structural systems

The architecture belongs to a class I will call **neural-structural**, named to distinguish it from neuro-symbolic AI. In a neuro-symbolic system, a neural component reasons over a fixed symbolic layer. In a neural-structural system, a discrete structure and a continuous representation co-evolve: the structure changes through a controlled vocabulary of mutation operations (Section 4), the continuous representation (embeddings, traversal weights) updates alongside it, the two share a learning signal, and each constrains the other. The structure is not a static scaffold the network consults; it is a participant in learning.

The concrete instantiation is a tool graph over the MCP ecosystem. The Model Context Protocol has, in a short time, made an enormous surface of capability reachable by agents [3]. The prevailing consumption pattern, however, is injection: connecting a server means serializing its tools into the agent's context. Practitioners have documented where this leads: tens of servers become hundreds of tool descriptions occupying the prompt, and injected context degrades model performance while the protocol forces an all-or-nothing load [4]. The door has been opened, the doorway is crowded, and the room is too small.

The proposal is to load tools not into context but into a knowledge graph. The underlying graph is full-resolution RDF: when a new MCP server appears in the registry, the ingestion path writes as much as is necessary to fully describe the server and its tools, including additions to a slim concept layer (think of a lightweight ConceptNet [13]) that situates each capability among the concepts it serves. Above the substrate sit projections, including labeled-property-graph views, in which nodes take on roles: tool, capability concept, server, problem class. Agents interact with projections; the substrate retains everything.

[[FIGURE 2: Two provisioning patterns. Left: injection serializes every connected tool into the context, where it competes with the task. Right: the graph holds everything at full resolution, and each agent receives only a task-scoped projection.]]

Access is then a property of the graph, not of the context. The orchestrator provisions a subagent by selecting a projection and weighting its edges, and "pruning" the ecosystem for quality means down-weighting rather than deleting: the substrate can in principle hold every MCP server in existence, while any given traversal sees a small, task-relevant neighborhood.

An objection should be raised immediately, because I raised it against myself in the first notes for this paper: once edges carry learned weights that determine routing, is the ontology anything more than a complicated graph embedding? The answer this paper commits to is: it converges toward one, and that is the point. The difference between this structure and a dense embedding is not mathematical character but governability. This embedding is sparse, inspectable, hand-editable, and carries provenance. A weight can be audited, a route can be explained, a mistaken edge can be corrected by a human with a reason attached. Call it a governable embedding. In domains where authoritative sources of truth and auditability are not optional, that property is the difference between an architecture you can deploy and one you cannot.

One design constraint falls out of the experiment in Section 6 and deserves to be stated as a derivation rather than a preference: the query interface to the graph must be simple. If the central claim is that structure substitutes for model scale, then the interface must sit within the competence of a very small model. A graph that can only be consulted through sophisticated query construction has merely relocated the demand for intelligence, not reduced it.

## 4. The mutation vocabulary

A neural-structural system changes, and the discipline of the approach is that it changes only through a discrete, controlled vocabulary of mutation operations. This is where the largest share of fundamental research remains, and this section makes claims at three levels of confidence.

High confidence: the vocabulary's first concrete member, which I will call **handler crystallization**. Cagle observes that once a handler exists for a task (a stored query, a defined method, a known transformation), the model need not be re-invoked to perform that task again; the model's role shifts to building new handlers rather than repeating old ones, and the economics shift from making the model cheaper to needing the model less often [3]. In graph terms: when a traversal (a tool chain, a query plan, a delegation pattern) succeeds repeatedly, a mutation operation promotes it into a first-class stored edge or artifact. Deliberate traversal becomes structure. This is proceduralization, the mechanism by which practiced cognition becomes habit, and it is the cleanest engineering instantiation of metanoia in the sense above: the change is earned by repetition under a real signal before it is committed to structure.

[[FIGURE 5: Handler crystallization. A traversal that succeeds repeatedly under a real signal is promoted into structure: the deliberate path becomes a stored, first-class handler, and the model is thereafter needed only where no handler yet exists.]]

Medium confidence: the shape of the learning process. Task outcomes, escalations, and adversary findings constitute a reward-like signal; something akin to reinforcement learning updates both the traversal weights and, through the mutation vocabulary, the structure itself. The provenance trail of traversals and validations is then not compliance overhead but the training log.

Low confidence, stated as the co-evolution requirement rather than a solved design: structural mutation in isolation is likely insufficient. A graph whose shape changes while the continuous representation that reads it stays fixed has performed change without undergoing it; a representation that drifts while the structure stays fixed has changed without an anchor. The requirement is that mutation operations and representation updates share a signal and constrain one another. This is not my first encounter with that wall: the Holonic Neural Networks postmortem [8] reached it from the other side, where the structural ideas held up and the learning story fell down. The mutation-policy research proposed here is a re-approach to the same missing dynamics layer, with a narrower and more measurable target. How to satisfy the requirement is, at time of writing, an open problem, and Section 7 discusses a candidate realization honestly, including what it lacks.

The vocabulary also reframes the pruning question from the paper's opening notes. If effectiveness is always relative to a problem subset (Section 1), then pruning the tool graph by a scalar quality metric is a category error. The correct objective is coverage of the problem space with redundancy removed: diversity preservation, the same principle applied to tools that Section 1 applied to problem-solving policies. Two scales, one rule.

## 5. Position among neighbors

This work sits deliberately at the junction of several efforts, and its citation posture differs across them.

Cagle's recent argument for MCP's importance [3] is the closest neighbor and the natural point of departure. His case is that MCP makes every capability behind an authenticated endpoint newly reachable without bespoke integration, collapsing a layer that consumed the integration industry for decades; and he concedes that applications persist partly because they solve discoverability, since a person does not know what is possible until an interface shows them the menu. This paper begins where that one ends. When everything is reachable, the binding constraint moves from reachability to selection, and the concept graph of Section 3 is the machine-facing answer to the discoverability concession: a menu for agents. Cagle's grounding argument also runs in the same direction as the present one from a different origin. He reads the progression from raw model output through retrieval augmentation toward data-first architectures as a return of the grounding layer to deterministic processes that hand the model focused, constrained context. Section 6's hypothesis is that same mechanism pushed to a measurable conclusion.

The critical literature on MCP in production supplies the problem statement. Practitioners report that connecting servers means an all-or-nothing context load, that tool counts scale into the hundreds, and that injected context degrades model performance [4]. These accounts are independent of this paper's thesis and are cited as motivation, not endorsement.

Industry is converging on the adjacent claim from the data side. Databricks now argues that generic assistants stall on real decisions because context is scattered across systems, and its answer is a unified context layer: a living knowledge graph of business terms, entities, and relationships, with authority resolved by ranking signals such as usage and links to certified assets, and with answers and actions inheriting existing governance [9]. Two observations follow. First, ranked authority over a governed graph is the governable-embedding pattern asserted as product strategy; and the recommendation to anchor AI in a company-owned context layer precisely so that models can be swapped without losing ground truth is the model-independence claim that this paper proposes to measure rather than assert. Second, that layer unifies data context (what is true, what terms mean); the present work is the capability-side complement (what can be done, with what access), and an agent plainly needs both.

A third neighbor is the literature on knowledge graphs as world models for agents. AriGraph has an agent construct a graph world model integrating semantic and episodic memory while exploring, and shows it outperforming full-history, retrieval-augmented, and RL baselines on complex text-game tasks while the decision-making component is held identical across conditions [10]. A recent survey of graph-augmented agents catalogs the pattern, including cases where a knowledge-graph-based executor enables smaller language models to perform complex multi-hop reasoning [11]. WALL-E 2.0 aligns an LLM's priors to an environment by extracting action rules and graphs from exploration trajectories and compiling them into code that regulates the agent's policy [12]. Three connections matter here. The small-model results are prior evidence, from an adjacent domain, for the interaction this paper preregisters; the novelty claimed here is the controlled two-by-two on tool provisioning, not the general idea that structure helps small models. The semantic-plus-episodic split names something this architecture already contains: the provenance layer is the episodic memory of the capability world model, the substrate in which repeated episodes become detectable, which is the precondition for handler crystallization; WALL-E 2.0's compiled action rules are the same crystallization move in another register. And the framing exposes a limit honestly: those systems model the task environment, while this graph models the capability space, and today it supports selection rather than prediction. Section 7 returns to that gap.

Two further relationships require precision. The candidate realization discussed in Section 7 is built on Cagle's four-graph holonic RDF model, so with respect to that candidate, Cagle's work is lineage rather than related work. The W3C Holon Graph Community Group [7], by contrast, is related but independent: the group's effort to identify the characteristics of holonic graph systems may surface other candidate realizations, and the mutation research proposed here lies outside the group's intent, though the two efforts may prove mutually beneficial.

## 6. The experiment

The experimentation repository implements a two-by-two design, preregistered here in the specific sense that the predictions and failure conditions are published before results exist.

|  | In-context provisioning | Graph-mediated provisioning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Frontier-scale model** | C1 | C2 |
| **Sub-billion-parameter model** | C3 | C4 |

Under in-context provisioning, tool schemas are serialized into the model's context in the prevailing pattern. Under graph-mediated provisioning, the model consults the concept graph through the deliberately simple query interface of Section 3 and receives a task-scoped projection.

**Prediction, stated in advance.** The performance gap between the frontier and small models will be large under in-context provisioning (C1 versus C3) and small, or at minimum substantially reduced, under graph mediation (C2 versus C4). This is a predicted interaction effect, not a main effect. The claim is not merely that graph mediation helps; it is that graph mediation flattens the capability gradient, because it relocates work from parameters into structure. If the gap persists undiminished under graph mediation across the metric layers below, the central claim of this paper fails, and the failure will be published.

[[FIGURE 3: The preregistered prediction, drawn before any data exist. Under in-context provisioning the capability gap between frontier and sub-billion models is large; under graph mediation it narrows. The interaction (converging lines), not either main effect, is the claim under test.]]

**Layered metrics.** Aggregate task success is too coarse to locate where equalization occurs, so measurement is decomposed into three layers: tool discovery and selection accuracy; argument construction validity; and end-to-end task success. The advance prediction is that equalization appears most strongly at the selection layer and partially at the end-to-end layer, since a small model that selects correctly may still fail multi-step execution. Measuring only end-to-end outcomes could mask a real selection-level effect, which is precisely the effect the architecture targets.

**Confound controls.** Three are designed in from the start. Context capacity: a small model may fail the in-context condition trivially because the schemas do not fit its window, so the design includes a reduced tool subset that does fit, separating "cannot fit" from "cannot reason over what fits"; both outcomes support the thesis, but by different mechanisms that must not be conflated. Curation fairness: the graph embeds curation effort, so the in-context baseline receives equivalently well-organized tool descriptions, ensuring the experiment measures structure rather than effort. Instrumentation parity: decision nodes are logged identically in all conditions, so that traversal policies are observable per the evidence bar of Section 1. The isolation discipline follows the world-model memory literature: as in AriGraph's evaluations, only the layer under test varies, while the decision-making component is held identical across conditions [10].

**Harness.** The frontier model is reached over its API; the small model runs locally. The harness comprises registry ingestion into the RDF substrate, the projection layer, the query interface, a task suite spanning problem classes with varying tool requirements, and uniform decision-node logging. The repository is private during initial development; it will be released, with results, in a follow-up paper.

## 7. A candidate realization, and what remains open

The architecture requires a graph substrate with role-typed projections, a discrete mutation surface, provenance, and boundary-level access control. One candidate realization exists in my own prior work: holonic [6], a Python library for holonic knowledge graphs built on Cagle's four-graph model, in which every holon carries interior graphs (what is true inside), boundary graphs (what is allowed), projection graphs (what outsiders see), and context graphs (where this belongs).

The mapping is direct. An MCP server is a holon. Its interior holds the full tool schemas. Its boundary is where provisioning writes: the natural home of edge weights and access modulation, with the adversary maintaining its own boundary set over shared interiors. Its projections are literally what a subagent sees. Its context holds registry and concept-graph membership. Portals, stored queries carried as RDF and executed on traversal, give handler crystallization an existing implementation primitive, and the library's provenance layer records traversals and validations, which is most of the decision-node instrumentation Section 1 requires.

[[FIGURE 4: An MCP server as a holon. The four-graph model assigns each architectural need a home: full schemas in the interior, access weights on the boundary, the subagent's view as a projection, registry and concept membership in context. Portals give crystallized handlers a first-class primitive.]]

Honesty about maturity and gaps is part of the candidate framing. The library is pre-1.0 (v0.7.1 at time of writing) but spec-disciplined: requirements carry identifiers and acceptance criteria, changes are gated on evidence, design decisions are logged with owners and outcomes, and the test suite stands at several hundred tests. More important is what the library's own open-question log already names: the four layers exist structurally, but there is no dynamics layer. Mutations are event-triggered rather than driven by a learning signal, and validation shapes validate rather than transform. The gap between a mutation surface, which exists (nine discrete operations over holons, layers, and portals), and a mutation policy, which does not, is exactly the fundamental research this paper proposes. A separately logged question, whether coordination among plural orchestrators should follow a capture-target-bubble event-propagation model in which "eventually ignored" is a legitimate outcome, is a live candidate for the trialogue's delegation semantics.

holonic is offered as a candidate, not a commitment. The Community Group's characteristics work may surface other realizations, and the architecture is defined by its requirements rather than by this implementation.

What remains open, in rough order of importance: the mutation policy and the co-evolution requirement (Section 4's low-confidence claim); a formal treatment of diversity-preserving pruning, including what coverage of a problem space means operationally; the bootstrapping question of whether the registry graph is itself a holon; and the formal relationship between a governable embedding and a learned dense embedding, including whether the former can be distilled from the latter without losing auditability. The world-model literature adds one more: whether tools should carry transition semantics (preconditions and effects), so that the capability graph supports prediction and planning rather than selection alone; that extension would connect the mutation vocabulary directly to the graph-as-state-machine question already logged against the candidate library [5, 10]. Adjacent to it sits the question of intent: a tool's declared semantics (its self-description) and its enacted semantics (what the provenance corpus shows it doing) deserve distinct edge types in the concept graph, because their divergence is itself a signal, and auditing it is a natural standing assignment for the adversary. Opacity makes enacted intent unprovable rather than uninterpretable: schema structure, provenance, and relational context all carry evidence, with a verifiable lower bound (behaviors witnessed) and an unverifiable upper bound (behaviors that cannot be ruled out). The graph then learns what tools are from what they do rather than what they say, which is the paper's account of change applied to the tools themselves. And where functionally equivalent tools differ on competing performance qualities, the pruning question resolves into dominance: dominated tools can be down-weighted without context, the non-dominated set must be preserved, and scalarizing the Pareto front becomes task-time provisioning work rather than graph-time curation, with the exploration of neglected non-dominated alternatives falling naturally to the adversary.
